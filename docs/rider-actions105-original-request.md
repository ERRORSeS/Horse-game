# OXER TO OXER — RIDER DISCIPLINE, HORSE REACTIONS & WELFARE CONSEQUENCES 9.0

## New Punish Horse Action • Spurs / Crop / Mouth • Individual Horse Responses • Repeated-Action Tracking • Temporary Compliance • Defensive Behaviors • Welfare Indicators • Detailed Training Reports

**CODEX IMPLEMENTATION PROMPT — INTEGRATE INTO THE EXISTING TRAINING AND HORSE-BEHAVIOR SYSTEMS**

# 0. MAIN OBJECTIVE

Add a new optional action in the existing training interface:

**Punish Horse**

The purpose is to simulate the behavioral and welfare consequences of punitive riding decisions, including the difference between temporary compliance, genuine understanding, and escalating distress.

This mechanic must be horse-dependent and integrated with the existing temperament, learned behavior, training and medical systems.

Do not build a separate training simulator.

Preserve all existing training exercises, horse attributes, personality traits, rider skills, training reports, and save data.

---

# PART I — NEW TRAINING ACTION

## 1. PUNISH HORSE BUTTON

Add a button labeled:

**Punish Horse**

Selecting it opens a small panel with the following categories:

| Category | Meaning in the simulation |
|---|---|
| Spurs | Punitive use of leg equipment |
| Crop | Punitive use of a riding crop |
| Mouth | Punitive rein/bit-related pressure |

These are gameplay categories, not instructions for using equipment.

The panel should also allow the player to classify the action as:

- Mild
- Forceful
- Excessive

These classifications represent the simulated severity of a recorded event. Do not supply physical technique instructions, force measurements, or optimization guidance.

Include a separate **Regroup / Release Pressure** action that allows the rider to return to a calmer training approach.

## 1.1 Equipment compatibility

Use the existing tack system.

- Spurs category requires the relevant equipped item.
- Crop category requires the relevant equipped item.
- Mouth-related effects must respect the horse's actual bitted or bitless configuration.
- Do not claim a bit caused discomfort when no bit is equipped.
- Do not create new tack items.

# PART II — IMMEDIATE HORSE RESPONSES

## 2. TEMPORARY COMPLIANCE IS NOT LEARNING

A horse may briefly respond more quickly following an aversive action.

However, this should not automatically count as successful training.

Possible immediate outcomes:

- Brief increase in responsiveness.
- No observable improvement.
- Tension.
- Loss of rhythm.
- Reduced focus.
- Hesitation.
- Distrust.
- Avoidance.
- Increased sensitivity.
- Defensive behavior.

A brief response can coexist with worse overall training quality.

For example:

A horse may initially accelerate but then become tense, lose rhythm, and perform the following exercise less accurately.

The training report must distinguish:

**Immediate response:** Briefly quicker.

**Actual exercise quality:** Reduced.

**Long-term learning:** No improvement.

Do not convert fear-based responses into permanent technical skill gains.

## 3.1 Different personalities

**Sensitive horse**

May show noticeable tension or withdrawal following a single incident.

**Hot-Blooded horse**

May become more reactive, frustrated or difficult to settle.

**Patient horse**

May initially remain outwardly cooperative, although that does not mean the experience has no welfare effects.

**Spooky horse**

May become more alert or reactive to the surrounding environment.

**Stubborn horse**

May disengage, resist the requested task or become less responsive.

**Highly trusting horse**

May initially continue cooperating, but repeated negative experiences can damage its existing trust.

These are tendencies, not guaranteed reactions.

Do not make a horse's response completely predictable from one personality label.

# PART III — REPEATED ACTIONS

## 4. TRACK EVERY ACTION

The player may select the action repeatedly during a session.

Record the repeated selections in the training history.

However:

**Repeated punishment must not stack unlimited performance bonuses.**

Track:

- Action category.
- Severity classification.
- In-game timestamp.
- Session ID.
- Previous related actions.
- Horse's current state.
- Actual immediate response.
- Subsequent training effects.
- Welfare concerns.

Repeated actions should influence the horse's ongoing emotional and behavioral state through the existing training model.

Avoid creating a separate arbitrary punishment-tolerance meter.

## 4.1 Horse response variation

Some horses may exhibit distress after one negative encounter.

Other horses may initially appear relatively unaffected but become increasingly tense during repeated incidents.

Do not use a deterministic threshold such as:

"Exactly five crop uses always causes rearing."

Responses should depend on the actual individual horse and current situation.

## 4.2 Escalation and welfare safeguards

If repeated actions produce serious distress:

- Stop awarding any temporary compliance effect.
- Allow relevant defensive or avoidance responses.
- Record the welfare concern.
- Prevent further punitive actions while the horse is in a serious unresolved distress state.
- Apply appropriate training restrictions under the existing welfare system.
- Allow recovery through appropriate care and positive training experiences.

Do not make repeated harmful inputs an unlimited progression mechanic.

# PART IV — DEFENSIVE BEHAVIOR

## 5. HORSES MAY REACT DEFENSIVELY

Possible behavioral outcomes include:

- Tail swishing.
- Ears pinned.
- Head raised.
- Stiffening.
- Loss of rhythm.
- Refusing to move forward.
- Moving away from pressure.
- Becoming distracted.
- Bucking.
- Rearing.
- Stopping the exercise.
- Difficulty settling afterward.

Use existing behavior and incident systems wherever possible.

Do not make bucking or rearing obligatory.

Do not treat defensive behavior as a personality defect.

The report should accurately describe the horse's observable response without claiming certainty about its thoughts.

## 5.1 Rider consequences

If an existing resolved behavioral incident affects rider balance or control, use the current rider skill and incident systems.

Do not introduce a second independent rider-fall roll.

Do not narrate a rider falling unless the actual simulation resolves a fall.

# PART V — WELFARE AND MEDICAL RECORDS

## 6. VISIBLE WELFARE CONCERNS

Where the existing health system supports them, track non-graphic indicators such as:

- Spur-area irritation.
- Mouth soreness or pressure-related marks.
- Equipment-associated discomfort.
- Increased sensitivity to contact.
- Stress-related training difficulties.

Do not automatically generate marks after every action.

Do not invent detailed injury descriptions or visual depictions.

These conditions must arise only when the relevant welfare or medical event is actually resolved.

## 6.1 Medical follow-up

When a medical concern is detected:

- Add it to the existing horse health record.
- Apply appropriate existing veterinary assessment rules.
- Respect necessary rest and training restrictions.
- Record any actual treatment.
- Avoid automatic instant recovery.

Do not create a second injury system.

## 6.2 Training report

Include a section:

**Rider Actions & Horse Welfare**

Example:

Action: Crop — Forceful  
Immediate response: Briefly quicker  
Training quality: Reduced rhythm and focus  
Horse response: Increased tension  
Long-term skill gain: None  
Welfare status: Monitor sensitivity

Only show effects that actually occurred.

# PART VI — DETAILED TRAINING NARRATIVES

## 7. NARRATIVE RULES

Create varied narrative lines for each action category.

Every narrative must correspond to the actual resolved horse response.

Use multiple sentences where helpful.

Do not randomly describe:
- Rearing when the horse did not rear.
- Bucking when the horse did not buck.
- Marks when none were recorded.
- Improved performance when the exercise quality did not improve.

Narratives should reflect the horse's existing personality, mood, experience, and current training.

Avoid repetitive identical sentences.

The following lines are examples of observable outcomes, not instructions.

# 8. SPURS — 12 TRAINING NARRATIVES

**1. Brief responsiveness**

"The horse responds more promptly for several strides, but the improvement is short-lived. Its rhythm remains less consistent than before, and the exercise finishes without a measurable training gain."

**2. Sensitive horse**

"The horse becomes noticeably tense following the rider's decision. Its attention shifts away from the original exercise, and the next transition lacks the smoothness it showed earlier."

**3. No improvement**

"Despite the additional pressure, the horse's response does not improve. It continues at much the same pace, with no meaningful change in the quality of the exercise."

**4. Frustration**

"The horse swishes its tail repeatedly and becomes less settled. Its reactions are quicker but less organized, making the following exercise harder to perform accurately."

**5. Loss of rhythm**

"The horse initially moves forward with greater urgency. Within moments, however, its rhythm becomes uneven, and the rider loses the consistency established earlier in the session."

**6. Loss of trust**

"The horse becomes increasingly cautious about responding to the rider's aids. Although it completes the exercise, the partnership shows less relaxation and willingness than before."

**7. Defensive response — bucking**

"The horse reacts defensively with a buck, interrupting the exercise. The session pauses while the situation is assessed, and the incident is recorded in the training report."

**8. Defensive response — rearing**

"The horse rears during a moment of heightened tension. The exercise cannot continue normally, and the training report records the defensive response and any resulting restrictions."

**9. Patient horse**

"The horse continues performing the requested movement without an obvious outward reaction. Nevertheless, the interaction does not create additional learning or justify an automatic improvement in its skills."

**10. Already anxious horse**

"The horse was unsettled before the action and becomes more distracted afterward. Its attention moves between the rider and the arena surroundings, reducing the quality of its work."

**11. Repeated incidents**

"As the session continues, the horse becomes progressively less comfortable with the interaction. Responses grow less consistent, and the earlier improvement in forwardness disappears."

**12. Recovery after regrouping**

"After the rider changes approach and allows the horse to settle, its rhythm gradually becomes steadier. The horse completes an easier exercise more calmly, although the earlier negative experience remains part of the session record."

# 9. CROP — 12 TRAINING NARRATIVES

**1. Brief reaction**

"The horse responds quickly, but the change is more abrupt than controlled. It takes several strides to recover a regular rhythm."

**2. No meaningful difference**

"The action produces no significant improvement in the horse's performance. Its pace and accuracy remain largely unchanged."

**3. Startled response**

"The horse becomes startled and loses concentration on the exercise. The next movement is less accurate as it attempts to settle."

**4. Frustrated horse**

"The horse pins its ears and becomes visibly frustrated. Its willingness to repeat the exercise decreases during the following attempt."

**5. Loss of relaxation**

"The horse continues forward but carries noticeably more tension. The movement is completed, though with reduced relaxation and consistency."

**6. Previously cooperative horse**

"The horse had been working steadily before the incident. Following the action, it becomes more hesitant during transitions and less confident about the rider's expectations."

**7. Defensive buck**

"The horse bucks and interrupts the exercise. The report records the defensive reaction instead of counting the interrupted attempt as successful training."

**8. Defensive rear**

"The horse reacts by rearing, bringing the exercise to an immediate interruption. Its current condition and the safety of continuing are assessed before further work."

**9. Repetition**

"Following repeated incidents, the horse no longer responds with the same initial urgency. Its focus deteriorates, and the session becomes increasingly unproductive."

**10. Distracted horse**

"The horse was already struggling to concentrate. The additional pressure further disrupts its attention, and it performs the next movement with reduced accuracy."

**11. Experienced horse**

"The experienced horse continues through the exercise, but the report identifies no improvement in its understanding or technical performance."

**12. Calmer return to work**

"Once the rider returns to clearer, calmer communication, the horse gradually resumes a more regular pace. The later improvement is recorded as part of the changed approach rather than attributed to the earlier punishment."

---

# 10. MOUTH — 12 TRAINING NARRATIVES

**1. Loss of contact quality**

"The horse becomes less settled in the contact. Its head carriage changes, and the following movement loses some of its earlier balance and consistency."

**2. Brief compliance**

"The horse initially responds more quickly, but the movement becomes less fluid. The apparent improvement does not translate into a higher-quality exercise."

**3. Sensitive horse**

"The horse shows heightened sensitivity around the contact and becomes reluctant to maintain a steady connection. Its next transition is less harmonious."

**4. No improvement**

"The horse's response remains essentially unchanged. The report records the rider's action but identifies no measurable improvement in training."

**5. Disrupted transition**

"The horse loses its previously steady rhythm during the transition. Although it completes the movement, the execution is less accurate."

**6. Increased tension**

"The horse becomes tense and less consistent in its responses. Its attention is divided, making it harder to return to the earlier working rhythm."

**7. Avoidance**

"The horse begins avoiding a steady connection, and the quality of the contact deteriorates. The next exercise is completed with reduced relaxation."

**8. Experienced dressage horse**

"The horse maintains the required movement but shows a decline in harmony and contact quality. Technical completion does not compensate for the loss of relaxation."

**9. Repeated incidents**

"Repeated negative interactions are followed by increasingly inconsistent responses. The horse becomes harder to settle, and the session's overall training quality declines."

**10. Defensive behavior**

"The horse reacts defensively and interrupts the requested movement. The response is recorded as a genuine training incident rather than a failure of willingness."

**11. Welfare concern**

"The horse displays signs of discomfort associated with the contact. The training report records the concern and refers it to the existing welfare assessment system."

**12. Improved communication afterward**

"Following a return to gentler, more consistent communication, the horse gradually regains a steadier contact. The improvement belongs to the subsequent training approach, not the earlier punitive action."

# PART VIII — INTEGRATION WITH EXISTING SYSTEMS

## 12. EXISTING LEARNED BEHAVIORS

Connect the mechanic to relevant existing fields, where actual eligible events justify changes:

- Rider Trust in Aids.
- Flatwork Confidence.
- Jump Confidence.
- Learned Spook Sensitivity.
- Handling Confidence.
- Training Anticipation.
- Relevant exercise-specific confidence.

Do not create a second trust system.

Do not automatically change every learned behavior after one action.

Do not increase technical skills because a horse briefly responded under pressure.

## 12.1 Competition consequences

If a horse carries unresolved tension into a later competition, use existing confidence and tension mechanics.

Do not assign arbitrary permanent competition penalties.

Only genuine persistent state changes should matter.

## 12.2 Horse individuality

The same category of rider action should not produce identical outcomes for every horse.

Use existing personality, experience and current state to determine plausible reactions.

Do not hardcode one behavioral result for all sensitive horses or all hot-blooded horses.

## 12.3 Safeguards

The simulation should not encourage escalating mistreatment as the optimal training method.

- No unlimited temporary performance boosts.
- No hidden bonuses for excessive punishment.
- No permanent skill gains from fear-based compliance.
- No reward for deliberately producing welfare problems.
- Serious welfare concerns use existing training and medical restrictions.
- Alternative calmer training approaches remain available.
